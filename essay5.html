Why we do what we do: the biology behind human behaviour

Besir Zeneli
24 November 2026

Imagine a moment. A student slams the classroom door. Another one helps a classmate pick up dropped books. A third freezes in the middle of an exam. We usually explain these moments with one word: anger, kindness, anxiety. Psychology names the behaviour. Biology asks what physically made it happen. For a long time the two subjects sat in separate buildings. The neurobiologist Robert Sapolsky has spent his career showing that they are two descriptions of the same story.

Turning the camera backwards

In his book Behave (2017), Sapolsky uses a simple method. Take any act and ask: what happened one second before? Which neurons fired? Then step further back. What did the person see, hear or smell in the minutes before? Which hormones were acting during the previous hours and days? How had the brain changed during the previous months? What happened in adolescence, in childhood, even in the womb? Which genes were involved, what culture shaped the person, and what did evolution build over millions of years?

The method is powerful because it removes a false choice. The question is never “is it biology or environment?” Every level shapes the next one. An experience becomes a change in neurons; a hormone changes how we react to an experience; a culture decides which experiences we have in the first place.

The alarm and the brake

Two brain regions appear again and again. The amygdala, deep in the temporal lobe, reacts fast to threat. It is the brain’s alarm. The prefrontal cortex, behind the forehead, holds back the impulses that would make us do something we regret. Sapolsky describes its job as making us do the harder thing when it is the right thing.

This is where psychology meets anatomy. What a teacher calls “self-control” has an address. And like any tissue, the prefrontal cortex can be tired: when we are hungry, sleep-deprived or stressed, it works less well, and the alarm wins more often. It is also the last part of the brain to finish maturing, usually in the mid-twenties. Teenagers are not simply “badly behaved”; they are using a brake that is still being built.

Hormones change the volume, not the song

Hormones act over hours and days. They rarely create a behaviour; more often they amplify what is already there. Testosterone, for example, does not make a calm person aggressive out of nowhere. It tends to strengthen whatever behaviour earns status in that situation. Oxytocin, often called the “love hormone”, increases trust and bonding, but mostly towards people we see as part of our own group.

The clearest example is stress. When the amygdala sounds the alarm, the hypothalamus, the pituitary and the adrenal glands release cortisol and adrenaline. Glucose rises and the heart beats faster: perfect for running from a lion. Sapolsky’s famous point is that zebras don’t get ulcers, because their stress lasts minutes. Humans can switch the same response on for months, just by thinking about rent, exams or the future.

Chronic stress has measurable effects on the brain. It can weaken the hippocampus, which is important for memory, and strengthen the amygdala. The result is a brain that learns less and fears more. Sapolsky’s years of field work on baboons in Kenya showed something similar: the health of an animal depended strongly on its social rank and on how much control and support it had.

Early life and genes

Further back, childhood leaves deep marks. Children who grow up with chronic stress, poverty or neglect often develop a more reactive stress system and a slower-maturing prefrontal cortex. This is not a moral failing; it is biology responding to an environment.

Genes matter too, but rarely in the way people imagine. There is no gene “for” aggression or kindness. Most genes change how sensitive we are to the environment. The same version of a gene can lead to problems in a harsh childhood and to no problems at all in a supportive one. Genes load the dice; the environment decides how often they are thrown.

Culture and evolution

At the widest level, culture tells us which behaviours are honoured and which are shameful, and evolution explains why our species has these brains, hormones and social instincts at all. We evolved as a social primate. Cooperation, fairness and empathy are as much part of that inheritance as fear and aggression.

What this means for us

Sapolsky pushes his argument very far. In Determined (2023) he concludes that, once all these causes are counted, there is no room left for free will. Many philosophers and scientists disagree, and the debate is far from settled. But even if we stop before his final conclusion, the framework changes how we look at people.

For a teacher, it is a lesson in humility. The student who slammed the door may have slept four hours, faced a crisis at home, or carried years of stress in a body that has learned to expect danger. Understanding the biology is not the same as excusing the behaviour. It is a better starting point for changing it: more sleep, less fear, more support, and a classroom where the brake has a chance to work.

The key idea: behaviour is never caused by a single thing. It is the last link in a chain that runs from neurons and hormones to childhood, culture and evolution. Psychology and biology are two ways of reading the same chain.

Further reading: Robert M. Sapolsky, Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst (Penguin Press, 2017); Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will (Penguin Press, 2023); Why Zebras Don’t Get Ulcers (3rd ed., 2004).
